How Many Custom Cups Should You Order? The Arithmetic Behind the Minimum

Quick Answer

The number you should order is not the minimum your supplier will accept. It is the quantity at which the fixed cost per piece stops being the reason your unit price looks high — plus enough spares to cover the cups that will be lost before the last guest leaves.

The rule is: order in the smallest quantity that removes the setup premium, then add 8–12% spares. Below that point you are paying for screens, colour purges and machine time that get spread over too few pieces. Far above it you are paying for storage, dead stock and cash that sits in a warehouse.

This page sets out the minimums that hide behind one quote, where the familiar 10 / 1,000 / 3,000 numbers come from, how the price actually behaves as quantity rises, how many spares an event needs by type, and when ordering more becomes the expensive choice. Numbers marked illustrative are methods, not our price list.

What Does “Minimum Order Quantity” Actually Mean?

One MOQ number usually conceals three different minimums, and buyers get caught by the second and third. Ask which one you are being quoted against before you negotiate.

The minimum you are being quotedWhat sets itWhat changes it
Per order — the smallest shipment the factory will scheduleMachine time: one run, one setup, one QC passWhether the model is already in production that week
Per colour — the smallest quantity in one PantoneColour change: purge material, clean the barrel, re-check against the chipWhether you accept a nearby stock colour instead of an exact match
Per decoration — the smallest quantity per printScreen, pad or plate setup, plus registration and first-off approvalWhether artwork is repeated from a previous order, in which case the setup already exists
Per mould — the smallest quantity that justifies new toolingSteel cost divided across the expected life of the toolWhether the shape already exists in the supplier's catalogue
If a supplier quotes “MOQ 1,000” without saying which of these it refers to, the real constraint is usually decoration, not the cup.

The practical consequence: three colours of the same cup at 300 pieces each is not the same order as 900 pieces in one colour. It is three colour changes and three print setups, and it is priced that way. Buyers who plan a colour range without knowing this are surprised at the sample stage, which is the most expensive place to be surprised.

Where Do 10, 1,000 and 3,000 Pieces Come From?

Those numbers are not marketing. Each one is the point at which a different cost stops dominating the unit price. They map onto the three routes to a custom cup, with the tooling route split by how much of the product you change.

RouteTypical entryWhat is being paid forWhat you get
Existing mould, any print or body colourFrom 10 piecesSetup only: screens, ink matching, a colour purgeYour artwork in your exact colour on a shape that already exists
Existing body, one new part — lid, cap, handle, baseFrom 1,000 piecesOne small mould, quoted on its ownA part that is not in the library
New shape or new productFrom 3,000 pieces per shapeSteel, cavity set, trial rounds, tool maintenanceA body, lid or closure that is yours
Entry quantities are the point where the route becomes sensible, not a hard wall. A 6,000-piece tooling order is possible; it just costs more per piece than a 20,000-piece one.

The 10-piece entry exists because a single-colour print on an existing model needs maybe twenty minutes of setup. There is no 250 tier any more: on a shape we already mould, the run starts at 10 whatever the number of colours. What used to force a 250-piece minimum — ink bought by the batch, a full purge of the ink path between colours — is now quoted as a setup cost on top of the unit price, because we would rather charge you for the setup than make you warehouse cups you do not need. The 1,000 and the 3,000 exist because a mould is a five-figure capital item: folded into the unit price it makes a short run absurd, so we quote the tooling as its own line and set the run at the quantity where the part price is still sensible — 1,000 pieces for one new part, 3,000 for a whole new product.

Worked example — illustrative figures only
Decoration setup (hypothetical)USD 180
Order 100 piecesUSD 1.80 per piece of setup
Order 500 piecesUSD 0.36 per piece
Order 2,000 piecesUSD 0.09 per piece
Order 2,000 pieces in four coloursUSD 0.36 per piece again — four setups
Setup does not scale down. Whatever the fixed amount is, dividing it by a small quantity is what makes a small order look expensive.

What Does the Price Actually Do as Quantity Rises?

It falls, but not in a straight line, and it stops falling sooner than most buyers expect. The first jump — from a few hundred to a couple of thousand — is large. After that each step saves less, because you have already absorbed the setup and are now only saving machine time and material purchasing.

Quantity bandWhat dominates the priceWhat a further increase savesWatch for
10 – 100Setup per order, picking, packingAlmost everything — setup is most of the costPer-unit price looks alarming and means nothing yet
100 – 500Setup still visible, material at retail-ish ratesA large step down, often 25–40%Colour changes quietly multiplying the setup
500 – 2,000Machine time, material, decorationA clear step down, then flatteningStorage and spares start to matter more than price
2,000 – 10,000Material, run speed, freight weightModest, single-digit percentages per stepCash tied up; artwork revisions become costly
10,000 +Material and freight onlyLittle to nothingYou are now buying a mould-shaped commitment in stock
Bands are indicative. The shape of the curve, not the exact percentages, is the useful part — it is the same curve on almost every plastic part.

Two things break the curve and are worth asking about. First, whether the factory is already running that model and colour this month — joining an existing run removes a setup you would otherwise pay for. Second, whether your artwork fits the print method without extra passes: a two-colour design on a curved surface can need two registrations, which is a second setup.

How Do You Find the Point Where More Pieces Stop Helping?

Divide the fixed costs by the quantity, add the variable cost, and compare the result against the price you need for your retail or event budget. When the fixed part per piece falls below roughly 10% of the target unit cost, quantity is no longer your problem.

Break-even sketch — illustrative figures only
Fixed costs (setup + plate/screen + sample)USD 620
Variable cost per cupUSD 0.85
Target landed cost per cupUSD 1.15
Headroom for fixed cost per cupUSD 0.30
620 ÷ 0.30 = quantity needed2,067 pieces
At about 2,000 pieces the fixed cost stops being the reason the cup is expensive. Below that, every extra 100 pieces buys a visible saving; above it, the saving is small enough that freight and storage can cancel it.

Run this before you ask for a quote, not after. A buyer who arrives with a quantity that already clears the fixed cost gets a cleaner number, and a supplier who can see the arithmetic is less likely to pad the range to protect themselves.

How Many Extra Cups Do You Actually Need?

Events lose cups in predictable ways: breakage in transit, staff using them behind the bar, guests taking them home, and the last-minute guest who was not on the list. The percentage depends on the setting, and it is more than most planners budget.

SettingSpares to addWhere they goNotes
Wedding or private party, seated service8 – 10%Breakage, staff, keepsakesGuests often take the cup home; count it as a giveaway, not a loss
Corporate event or conference10 – 12%Walk-ins, staff, damaged in transitBranded cups disappear fastest at multi-day events
Bar, club or festival, high volume12 – 15%Drop damage, clearing, theftOrder in the same colour as the venue's backup stock if possible
Stadium or sports venue10 – 15%Concourse loss, stacking damageStacking damage is real: badly stacked cups crack at the rim
Sample and approval stage10 – 20 piecesYour team, the client, the file copyAlways keep one signed reference piece per colour
These are planning percentages from event work, not guarantees. The test is simple: how many cups were short at your last event, and what did the shortfall cost at the bar?

Two spares categories get forgotten. The first is the reference set: one approved cup per colour and per print, kept out of circulation so a repeat order can be matched against something physical rather than a photograph. The second is the replacement set for the venue — cups that will be in use next season, ordered in the same run so the colour matches.

What Happens When You Split One Order Across Colours or Sizes?

Splitting does not divide the minimum, it multiplies it. Each additional colour or size is another changeover, and the changeover is what the minimum was protecting.

SplitWhat the factory has to doEffect on your unit price
Two colours, existing mould, same print methodOne extra colour change and purgeSetup roughly doubles for that order
Two colours with different artworkExtra purge plus extra print setupSetup doubles and approval doubles
Two sizes of the same bodyUsually no extra mould; different cycle and packingSmall, mainly packing and carton count
Two lids, one bodySecond part to stock, match and packSmall if the lid already exists; new lid means a new minimum
One colour, one print, one sizeNothing extraThe cheapest configuration you can buy
If you need a range, the cheapest structure is usually a shared base colour with a small number of accent variations, not four equal colour splits.

There is a legitimate reason to split anyway: a distributor needs a sellable range, not one colour. In that case, split by print rather than by cup colour where possible — the cup can be printed in different colours from a single base stock, which is one mould, one material batch and one colour change.

When Does Ordering Less Cost More?

Small orders are cheap per decision and expensive per cup. Three costs hide in them: the setup you pay in full, the freight rate you lose by shipping a light carton, and the second setup when you run short.

  • You pay the setup twice. A shortfall after the first delivery usually means a second small run, and the second run carries a second full setup. The combined cost is often more than the original larger order.
  • Freight is priced per kilogram and per cubic metre, but with a floor. A 300-cup shipment occupies almost the same container slot as 600 and pays almost the same handling, so the freight per cup roughly halves.
  • Production slots are not equal. A tiny order may be scheduled around bigger runs, which can add days. Ask where a small order sits in the queue before you assume it will ship faster.
  • Sample costs are not recoverable. A sample you pay for on a 200-piece order is a much larger share of the total than the same sample on 2,000 pieces.

When Does Ordering More Cost More?

Above the break-even point, every extra pallet is a cost that never appears on the invoice: storage, insurance, dead stock, and the colour drift between an old batch and a new one.

Cost that grows with quantityHow it shows upHow to keep it small
StoragePallet space, rent, re-handling, damp cartonsAgree a delivery schedule instead of one drop; ask whether stock can be held at the factory
Dead stockCups from a campaign that ended, a colour the client droppedBuy to a plan with a named use, not to a discount
Colour drift across batchesThe second half of a big order is slightly off the first halfAsk for one production batch where possible, or approve a tolerance up front
Cash and working capitalMoney in boxes instead of in the businessCompare the discount against the cost of holding for a year
Artwork obsolescenceLogo or date printed on 5,000 cups you cannot sellNever print a date or a season on the whole quantity
The last row is the one that hurts. Any cup carrying a date, a venue name or a sponsor should be ordered for that event only.

What This Article Cannot Tell You

It cannot give you a number, because the number depends on four things we do not know yet: the model, the decoration, the number of colours and the delivery address.

  • It does not tell you what a cup costs. Setup amounts, variable costs and freight rates in this page are illustrative, used to show the method.
  • It does not cover print registration quality or colour accuracy, which can change what quantity is sensible — read the colour guide before you approve a large run.
  • It does not account for your own spoilage after delivery: warehouse damage, transport between venues, or staff handling.
  • It does not know your sales curve. A distributor ordering for a season has a different right answer from an event organiser buying once.
  • It does not replace a written specification. Two quotes at the same quantity can differ by a third simply because one is a thinner wall.

What to Send Us to Get a Real Number

Six lines of information turn a range into a number. Send these and the answer usually comes back the same day:

  1. The model or the shape you have in mind, plus capacity in oz or ml.
  2. The quantity you think you need, and separately the date it has to be in hand.
  3. How many colours in the artwork, and where it sits on the cup.
  4. Whether colour must match a Pantone chip or a stock colour is acceptable.
  5. Whether you need lids, straws or packaging in the same shipment.
  6. The delivery city and whether it can travel by sea.

If the shape does not exist yet, quantity is the second question, not the first — start with the tooling guide. If the shape exists and you only need your logo on it, the ordering process explains what happens between your brief and your delivery date.
