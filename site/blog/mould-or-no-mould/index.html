Quick Answer

Most branded cups do not need a new mould. They need a new colour, a new print, or a different lid — three changes that use tooling we already own. A new mould is only the right answer when the body shape itself is the product, and when the volume is large enough that the tooling cost per unit stops mattering.

The decision rule is arithmetic, not taste. Divide the mould cost by your first-order quantity. If the answer is a large fraction of the unit price you are trying to hit, the mould is too early. If it is small enough that the landed cost still works against your retail price, the mould is justified. This single calculation decides more tooling projects than any design argument.

Below: what counts as existing tooling, the volume bands where each route pays for itself, what actually moves a mould price, how many cavities to specify, and the six stages between a drawing and a cup you can ship. All figures marked as illustrative are methods, not our price list.

What Counts as an Existing Mould?

Buyers often ask for a quote on a completely new mould when a colour change would have done the job. Before you go to tooling, check which of these three levels your change actually sits at. Suppliers should tell you this on the first call, not after you have paid a sample fee.

What you want to changeWhat has to be madeTooling neededEntry quantity
Body colourColour concentrate, then a production runNone — existing mouldFrom 10 pieces
Logo or artworkA screen, pad or laser filePrint tooling onlyFrom 10 pieces (250 for colour-changing inks)
Lid, handle or strawA separate small partSmall part mould, often already ownedFrom 1,000 pieces (10 if we already own that part mould)
Body shape, capacity or wallA new mould cavity setNew mouldFrom 3,000 pieces per shape
Thread, seal or closure systemNew mould for body and lid togetherNew mould, two halvesFrom 3,000 pieces per shape
The first three rows are how most branded drinkware programmes actually start. Only the last two are tooling projects.

There is a fourth case that catches people out: your cup exists, but the mould belongs to another customer. Exclusive tooling is common in this industry. Ask directly whether the mould for the model you are being quoted is free, shared, or customer-owned — the answer decides whether you can have it in your own colour at all.

When Does a New Mould Pay for Itself?

A mould is a fixed cost spread across units. The only question that matters is how many units. Work it in three lines.

Worked example — illustrative figures only
Mould cost (hypothetical figure for the method)USD 6,000
First order3,000 pieces
Tooling per piece (6,000 ÷ 3,000)USD 2.00
Second order at the same volumeUSD 1.00
Fifth order at the same volumeUSD 0.40
At a 3,000-piece first order, USD 6,000 of tooling is USD 2.00 per piece — which is exactly why we quote the tooling as its own line instead of hiding it in the cup price. Against a USD 4.00 target that would have been a 50% surcharge on the first order and 25% on the second; against USD 8.00, 25% and then 12.5%.

Two adjustments make the arithmetic honest. First, add the cost of the sample rounds and any engineering time the buyer has to absorb. Second, discount the later orders: an order you hope to place in year three is worth less than an order you place now, and a mould you cannot amortise within about three years is usually the wrong decision at your current volume.

Your situationUsual right answerWhy
First product, unproven demand, under 3,000 unitsExisting mould + your colour and printYou keep the option to change shape after the first sell-through
Brand already sells the same product shape, 10,000+ a yearOwn the mouldYou stop competing for shared capacity and protect the shape
Shape is the brand — the silhouette is the productOwn the mould at the lowest viable quantityA copied shape has no defensible advantage
Promotional or event order, one seasonExisting mould, decoratedTooling will never amortise inside the campaign
Buyer needs a size that does not exist in the catalogueNew mould, single cavity firstProve sell-through before paying for multi-cavity output

What Actually Drives the Price of a Mould?

Six factors explain almost all of the difference between two quotes for the same cup. If a quote is far below the others, one of them has been quietly dropped.

FactorWhat it changesEffect on price
Number of cavitiesOutput per cycle, and the size of the steel blockEach extra cavity adds cost roughly in steps, not proportionally — a 4-cavity is well under 4× a 1-cavity
Steel gradeTool life. P20 for tens of thousands of shots, hardened grades for hundreds of thousandsHardened steel and heat treatment add a meaningful premium
Sliders and liftersAny undercut: a recessed handle, a snap groove, an internal ribEach slider is a separate mechanism that must be fitted and tested
Surface finishTexture, gloss level, or a polished bandTexture is etched into the steel; changing it later means re-cutting
ToleranceWall thickness consistency, thread fit, lid sealTight tolerance means more trial rounds and slower cycles
Prototype route3D print, aluminium trial mould, or straight to production steelAluminium bridges the gap but is a cost you pay twice
Where buyers lose money

The expensive mistake is not choosing a cheap mould. It is changing the shape after the steel is cut. A wall thickness change, a thread change or an added undercut after hardening can cost most of the mould price again, and it restarts the timeline.

Bring every change before the design freeze — the freeze is the last cheap moment.

How Many Cavities Should the Mould Have?

Cavity count is a capacity decision disguised as a price decision. It sets how many pieces come off one machine per day, and therefore whether you can hit a delivery date without holding stock.

CavitiesShots per dayPieces per dayFits
11,7601,760Pilot runs, low volume, high-mix catalogue
21,7603,520The usual starting point for a branded cup
41,7607,040Repeat programmes with confirmed annual volume
81,76014,080Single-SKU, high-volume, price-sensitive
Cycle time is the other half of the equation. A thick-walled tumbler runs slower than a thin-walled cup, so the same cavity count yields less. Ask for the cycle time in writing, not just the cavity count.

The practical rule: match cavities to your repeat order, not your first order. A 4-cavity mould paid for by one launch order sits idle afterwards, and idle steel is money you cannot get back. Start at two cavities unless you have twelve months of confirmed volume.

Should You Start With a Bridge Mould?

A bridge mould is cut in aluminium instead of steel. It produces real parts — same shape, same wall, same material — but it wears out after a few thousand shots. It exists so you can test a market before committing to production steel.

Aluminium bridge mouldProduction steel mould
Tool lifeA few thousand shotsTens to hundreds of thousands of shots
Lead timeRoughly halfFull
Part qualityClose to production; surface finish slightly softerProduction finish
CostA fraction of steelFull
What it is forSell-through testing, trade shows, investor samplesRepeat production
The cost of a bridge mould is only worth it if it saves you from a wrong steel tool. If you already know the shape sells, the bridge is money spent twice.

Two honest warnings. A bridge mould is not a cheap way to get production parts — a run of 5,000 from aluminium will show wear on the split line and the texture. And not every shape can be bridged: thin walls, long cores and fine threads behave differently in aluminium, so a bridge that works does not guarantee the steel tool will behave identically.

What Happens Between a Drawing and a Shippable Cup?

Six stages. Each one ends with something you approve — and each approval is the point where a change is still cheap.

  1. Design review (DFM). We read your drawing for wall thickness, draft angle, undercuts, gate position and shrinkage. You get a written list of what will not mould cleanly and what it will cost to fix. Nothing is cut yet.
  2. 3D printed model. A physical shape in your hand, usually within a week. This is where ergonomics and proportion get settled — hold it, fill it, put it in a cup holder.
  3. Mould design sign-off. Cavity layout, split line, ejection, cooling and texture are drawn and sent for approval. Your signature here is what protects both sides later.
  4. Steel cutting and fitting. The longest stage. Trial shots begin as soon as the tool closes properly.
  5. Trial rounds (T1, T2…). First shots are checked for flash, short fill, sink marks, dimensions and colour. Most projects need two rounds; a difficult shape can take four.
  6. Sample approval and freeze. You approve a physical sample, we retain a signed reference piece, and the tool is frozen. From here, production runs repeat the approved sample.
Ask for this in writing

“The production tool will be built to the approved sample; any change to cavity count, steel grade or texture will be notified and quoted before it is made.” One sentence, and it prevents the most common tooling dispute.

StageWorking daysWhat you receive

Simple open shapes sit at the bottom of these ranges. A cup with a handle, an internal thread and a textured band sits at the top. Add transit time — the tool does not travel, but the samples do.

What Goes Wrong, and Who Pays?

  • Change after the freeze. Usually the buyer's cost. The exception is a change needed because the tool cannot produce the approved drawing — then it is ours, and it should be stated that way before the work starts.
  • Wear over time. Tools wear. A mould that has run several hundred thousand shots will start to show flash on the split line, and it needs maintenance, not a new mould. Ask what the expected shot life is and who maintains the tool.
  • Ownership. If you pay for the mould, say so in the contract and ask where it is stored. A mould you paid for and cannot retrieve is not an asset.
  • Capacity priority. Owning the mould does not automatically give you priority in a busy factory. Put a delivery commitment in the order, not just a price.

What Should You Put in a Mould Enquiry?

A useful enquiry gets a useful number. Vague ones get a wide range, and wide ranges are what make buyers think tooling is unpredictable. Send these seven things:

  1. A 2D drawing with dimensions and tolerances, or a 3D file (STEP preferred).
  2. Capacity in ml or oz, plus the filled weight if the product is sold by volume.
  3. Material and wall thickness, or the requirement that drives them (hot fill, dishwasher, freezer).
  4. Annual volume and first-order volume, stated separately.
  5. Every undercut, recess, thread or handle — with a sketch if it is unusual.
  6. Surface finish: gloss level, texture name or a physical reference.
  7. Any certification the finished part must meet, because it can change the material and therefore the shrinkage and the tool.

If the shape is not yet decided, the honest starting point is a development route rather than a mould quote: sketch, model, then tool. And if you only need your logo on an existing cup, none of this applies — read the customization guide instead.
